UX case study

L'Artusi Dine-In

Designing an order-and-pay app for a Greenwich Village Italian restaurant

At a glance

Ordering at the table, without the wait

Problem

Ordering and paying meant flagging down a server, leading to miscommunication, errors and long waits.

Goal

Let diners order and pay from their table, accurately and on their own time.

Outcome

A tested hi-fi prototype covering check-in, menu, customization, call-a-waiter and checkout.

My role

UX designer, end to end, from concept to high-fidelity prototype.

Timeline

June 2021 – January 2022

Methods

Interviews, empathy maps, personas, journey mapping, wireframes, prototypes, 2 usability rounds

The product

Modern Italian in the heart of the Village

L'Artusi is an Italian restaurant in Greenwich Village, New York, serving a modern take on traditional Italian cuisine that balances comfort and creativity.

Its guests come from all over the world, so the app had to make an Italian menu easy to understand, order from and pay for, in any language.

01

Understanding the user

Research summary · Pain points · Personas · Journey map

Research summary

Diners wanted more than speed. They wanted less contact.

I ran interviews and built empathy maps to understand who I was designing for. The primary user group: working adults who regularly ate out during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Research confirmed the initial assumptions about miscommunication and wait times, and surfaced a third factor: people were wary of new places and of interacting with strangers.

Assumed

Miscommunication with servers and long waits

Discovered

Fear of new places and of unnecessary contact with others

Pain points

Four problems to solve at the table

1 · Navigation

Mobile menus are hard to move through and find things in.

2 · Time

Long waits to be attended to when seated, and again at checkout.

3 · Mix-ups

Language barriers lead to more errors when orders are taken.

4 · Accessibility

Dishes are often named in another language, with little or no description.

Starting the design

Paper wireframes · Digital wireframes · Low-fidelity prototype · Usability studies

Paper wireframes

Sketch wide, then keep what solves a pain point

I drafted several versions of each screen on paper before going digital. On the item screen, I prioritized a photo and description of the dish, and the ability to adjust it for dietary preferences.

★ Starred elements made it to the final mockup.

Digital wireframes

Built to remove miscommunication

Every screen traced back to a research finding, with the heaviest focus on language barriers. Guidance mattered most in the final steps: placing the order and paying.

Usability studies

Two rounds of testing, each one changed the design

Round 1 · Wireframes → mockups

Users wanted an easier way to add more items to their cart. Users needed one intuitive flow to order and pay at the table. Onboarding and ordering needed clearer, more consistent guidance.

Round 2 · High-fidelity prototype

Users wanted to call their waiter at any moment. Customization toggles were confusing for pre-selected ingredients.

Accessibility

Designed for any diner, in any language

1 · Familiar icons

Commonly known icons help diners navigate and complete tasks without reading every label.

2 · Language switching

The app defaults to the system language, and diners can change it any time in settings.

3 · Consistent CTAs

High-contrast buttons in the same position on every page guide diners from one task to the next.

04

Going forward

Takeaways · Next steps · Looking back with a product lens

Takeaways

The first idea is a starting point, not the flow

Impact

The app meets diners' needs at the table and is built to reduce miscommunication between servers, kitchen staff and customers.

What I learned

Usability studies and peer feedback shaped every iteration and kept my own assumptions about a “common” experience in check.

Next steps

Where I'd take the research next

1 · Onboarding at check-in

Research how easily diners get started once they're checked in at the restaurant.

2 · Kitchen-to-table loop

Analyze kitchen feedback to make sure open-ended requests sent through the app are handled well.

Looking back · 2026

What I'd add today, with a product lens

Success metrics up front

Define what “faster and clearer” means: time from seating to order, order error rate, time to check out.

Both sides of the table

Map server and kitchen workflows too, so the app removes work for staff instead of moving it.

The business case

Test adoption and point-of-sale integration, and weigh the cost to the restaurant against table turnover.
